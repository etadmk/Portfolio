01 · Why the spot is 5 km

Derive this in the room and you have established more than any answer about your background could.

The spot is not set by the mirror. A flat mirror does not focus — it forms an image of the source, and the source is the Sun, which subtends about 0.53°, or 9.3 mrad. Reflect that off a flat surface and the beam diverges at the Sun's own angular width no matter how big the mirror is.

spot ≈ altitude × sun angular diameter ≈ 540 km × 9.3 mrad ≈ 5.0 km ← their published number so the quoted spot implies an orbit near 540 km

The follow-on is equally clean. Ground illuminance is solar illuminance scaled by the area ratio:

E ≈ E_sun × (A_mirror / A_spot) × reflectivity ≈ 100,000 lux × (324 / 1.96×10⁷) × 0.9 ≈ 1.5 lux full moon ≈ 0.25 lux → roughly 6 full moons

Their published claim is about four times a full moon inside the beam. Landing within a factor of two of a company's headline number with two lines of arithmetic is the strongest single thing you can do in a technical round.

02 · The pointing problem

This is the actual mechanical engineering, and it has two halves: how fast you must slew, and what the structure does when you try.

line-of-sight rate to a fixed ground point, overhead pass: ω = v/h = 7600 / 540,000 = 0.0141 rad/s = 0.81 °/s mirror rate = half of that ≈ 0.40 °/s momentum at that rate, I ≈ 430 kg·m²: H = Iω = 430 × 0.0070 ≈ 3.0 N·m·s

Half a degree per second sounds trivial until you multiply by the inertia. Three newton-metre-seconds is a serious wheel for a small satellite, and it has to be dumped between passes. That single number frames the whole ADCS conversation.

And the structure fights you

A tensioned membrane has almost no stiffness and almost no damping. Control bandwidth has to sit roughly a decade below the first mode, so you are steering a 430 kg·m² object with a loop that updates meaningfully every twenty seconds or so. Higher tension raises the mode but loads the booms and the film. That trade — tension versus boom mass versus achievable bandwidth — is the central structural argument of the vehicle.

03 · How flat does it have to be?

Less flat than people assume, and the number is worth knowing because it reframes the problem.

allow ~20% beam broadening on top of 9.3 mrad: √(11.2² − 9.3²) ≈ 6.2 mrad of slope-induced spread δ = 6.2/2 ≈ 3 mrad RMS slope error ≈ 0.18° for a wrinkle of 1 m wavelength that is ~0.5 mm amplitude

Millimetre flatness over eighteen metres. That is demanding for a tensioned polymer film but it is a structures and metrology requirement, not an optical-shop one. Saying that out loud shows you have calibrated the problem rather than assumed it is a telescope.

04 · Attitude is the ballistic coefficient

The non-obvious systems insight, and the one most likely to earn a real conversation.

With an areal density near 50 g/m², this vehicle has the ballistic coefficient of a solar sail. Deployed face-on it would decay in weeks. The company has publicly said the satellite defaults to a low-reflectivity orientation and tilts away when not serving a customer — that is usually framed as a light-pollution mitigation, and it is, but it is also mandatory for orbit life. Solar radiation pressure and drag are the same order of magnitude and both scale with projected area, so attitude is simultaneously the optical function, the disturbance environment and the orbit-maintenance strategy.

The disturbance torque nobody budgets for
Solar radiation pressure acts at the centre of pressure. Any offset from the centre of mass — from asymmetric deployment, a wrinkle, or a torn corner — turns 2.9 mN into a continuous torque. At a 0.5 m offset that is 1.5 mN·m, which accumulates roughly 8 N·m·s of momentum per orbit. Magnetorquers at 540 km give order 0.3 mN·m. The desaturation budget is genuinely tight, and asking about it is a strong question.

05 · Thermal and materials

Where your background gives you something concrete to say.

thermal capacity per area = 49 g/m² × 1200 J/kgK ≈ 59 J/m²K radiative h = 4εσT³ ≈ 3.2 W/m²K at 260 K τ = 59 / 3.2 ≈ 19 s the film reaches thermal equilibrium in under a minute

That is the thermal snap problem. Crossing the terminator, an 18 m membrane changes temperature almost instantly while the booms lag. With mylar at roughly 17 ppm/K, a 100 K swing moves 18 m by about 30 mm. If the booms are near-zero-CTE composite, that entire mismatch has to be absorbed by the tensioning system — constant-force springs or compliant corner fittings — or the film goes slack in eclipse and over-tensioned in sun. Slack film is wrinkled film, and wrinkled film is scattered light.

07 · Your bridges — and the honest gap

This is not a thermofluids company. Do not pretend otherwise; map what actually transfers.

  • High-speed imaging and OpenCV. At UIUC you extracted wave velocity from high-speed video of falling films. Deployment characterisation and membrane wrinkle metrology are the same discipline: get geometry and kinematics out of images. This is your strongest single bridge and almost nobody else will have it.
  • Test rig fabrication and DAQ. Deployment testing needs gravity offload rigs, instrumented fixtures and repeatable procedures. You have built and debugged rigs and separated instrumentation artefacts from real physics.
  • Surface and materials characterisation. SEM, wettability, coating behaviour, vacuum and glovebox work from Zap. Film handling and surface condition are central here.
  • Thermal cycling and CTE. You know the fatigue and mismatch story cold, and the 30 mm expansion problem is exactly that story on a membrane.
  • Your transient conduction solver. The membrane thermal transient at eclipse entry is a small, well-posed version of what you already wrote.
  • MarineSitu LP/MILP scheduling. A constellation serving customer requests while avoiding observatories, respecting sun-target-satellite geometry and slew limits is a constrained scheduling problem. At 50,000 satellites it is the software problem. Say this.
Handle the gap directly
You have not worked deployable structures or attitude control. Say so once, early, and pivot: "I haven't worked deployables — my experience is experimental thermal-fluids, test hardware and characterisation. What I'd bring on day one is rig building, instrumentation and getting real data out of a test article. I'd expect to be learning the structures side." That is a better answer than a thin claim, and it is true.

08 · Ask them

  • "How do you test deployment on the ground — gravity offload on a full 18 m article, or sub-scale plus analysis?"
  • "What sets the flatness requirement in practice — beam quality on target, or stray light outside it?"
  • "Is control bandwidth limited by the first membrane mode, and did that drive the tension level?"
  • "How much of the disturbance budget is solar radiation pressure torque from centre-of-pressure offset, and how do you desaturate?"
  • "Feathering when not serving reads as a light-pollution measure — is it also doing the work on drag and orbit life?"
  • "At 50,000 units, is tasking a scheduling problem you're already solving? My current work is a constrained scheduler for exactly that shape of problem."
  • "What broke on the first deployment test that wasn't in the model?"
