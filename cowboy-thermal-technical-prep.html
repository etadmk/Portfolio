01 · The architecture you should assume

If you sketch anything on a whiteboard, sketch this. It is the standard answer for a high-power spacecraft and it explains itself.

Why two loops and not one. Ammonia is the right external fluid — high latent heat, good vapour pressure in the 250–350 K band, decades of flight heritage. But ammonia attacks copper and copper alloys, and GPU cold plates are copper because you need the spreading conductivity at the die. So you run water or a glycol mix internally where the copper is, ammonia externally where the radiators are, and couple them through an interface heat exchanger. This is exactly the ISS architecture and it is the safe answer.

Say the cost out loud too: the interface heat exchanger adds a temperature drop you cannot recover, and every kelvin you spend there costs radiator area as the fourth power.

02 · Sizing the radiator

The single most likely whiteboard question. Have the equation and the fourth-power intuition ready before the number.

A = Q / (ε σ T⁴) Q = 1×10⁶ W ε = 0.85 σ = 5.67×10⁻⁸ at 320 K: σT⁴ = 594 W/m² → ×0.85 = 505 → A ≈ 1,980 m² at 350 K: σT⁴ = 851 W/m² → ×0.85 = 723 → A ≈ 1,383 m² at 400 K: σT⁴ = 1452 W/m² → ×0.85 = 1234 → A ≈ 810 m²

The point to make is not the number, it is the shape. Area is not a design choice, it is a consequence of the temperature you can hold. That reframes the whole conversation: the interesting engineering is in the ΔT budget between the die and the radiating surface, because every kelvin you give away there is paid for in deployable area, mass and stowed volume.

03 · The ΔT budget — where the argument actually is

If you bring one original framing into the room, bring this one.

Three drops, three different disciplines. The die-to-coolant drop is interface material, spreading resistance and cold-plate design. The heat exchanger and line drop is the price of the two-loop architecture. The fin and glide drop is radiator panel construction and whether the loop is single or two-phase. Naming which of the three you would attack first — and why — is a much better answer than a number.

04 · Single-phase or two-phase

Expect this. It has two independent arguments and most candidates only know one.

single-phase: ṁ = Q / (c_p ΔT) = 10⁶ / (4180 × 20) ≈ 12 kg/s two-phase: ṁ = Q / h_fg = 10⁶ / 1.1×10⁶ ≈ 0.9 kg/s roughly 13× less mass flow for the same megawatt

The mass-flow argument is the bigger one. Thirteen times less flow means smaller lines, less inventory, less pump power and less mass — at megawatt scale that is structural, not marginal. The isothermal argument is worth roughly 10–15% of area on top.

Say the cost, not just the benefit
Two-phase in microgravity gives up buoyancy. Bubbles do not depart the boiling surface on their own, so critical heat flux drops. Condensation becomes shear-driven rather than gravity-drained. Parallel evaporators can oscillate against each other. And none of it can be fully replicated in a 1 g test rig. If you name those unprompted, the two-phase answer sounds like judgment rather than a preference.

05 · Fluid system hardware — what is in the loop and why

The round is on hardware. Know what each component exists to prevent.

ComponentExists because
AccumulatorLiquid is nearly incompressible. A 30 K swing on a rigid water loop would move pressure by roughly 140 bar. It also sets pump inlet pressure and absorbs leakage over life.
Pump, redundantFive years is 43,800 hours continuous. Bearings and any dynamic seal are the life driver — wet-rotor brushless removes the seal entirely.
FilterManufacturing particulate blocks microchannels. Mesh sized below the smallest channel dimension, upstream of the cold plates.
Isolation valvesSegment the loop so one puncture does not empty the system.
Gas trap / ventNon-condensable gas migrates to the condenser and blocks area, driving the whole loop hotter.
HeatersAmmonia freezes at 195 K, water at 273 K. A radiator permanently facing cold space can freeze at low compute load.
Quick disconnectsIntegration and servicing without breaking the charge. Also the most likely leak path.
InstrumentationRTDs, pressure transducers, flow meters — and the honest question is which quantities are measured versus inferred.

Manifold distribution across 800 cold plates

Static pressure recovers along a supply header as velocity drops, so downstream branches can see a higher driving Δp than upstream ones. The fixes are header cross-section far larger than the sum of the branches, deliberate orificing to make branch resistance dominate, or reverse-return plumbing so every path has the same total length. Design to the worst channel, not the mean.

07 · Your bridges

Every one of these is on your résumé. Use the phrasing, not a paraphrase.

  • UCSD, CHF on capillary evaporators. A 1.25 kW GPU spread over a die is order 100–150 W/cm². You characterised evaporator surfaces well above that. Say: "the flux at the die is inside the range I've measured on capillary wicks — the difference here is that dryout means losing a GPU, not a data point."
  • UIUC, boiling on machined surfaces. Groove depth and spacing changed both nucleation and how much the surface degraded. Directly applicable to enhanced boiling surfaces in a two-phase cold plate — and you found that geometry has a threshold, which is a real result.
  • UIUC, crystallisation fouling. The closest thing you have to long-duration degradation of a heat transfer surface. In a closed loop the analogue is corrosion product and particulate slowly blocking microchannels.
  • Zap, liquid metal loops. Materials compatibility, freeze protection, eutectics chosen so nothing solidifies in a pipe, glovebox and vacuum handling. Loop engineering is loop engineering.
  • Your ManifoldWaterCooling 1D model. This is the most directly relevant thing you have built. Lead with it in any manifold or cold-plate conversation.
  • MarineSitu LP/MILP scheduling. Scheduling loads against a stored-energy and generation constraint. An orbital data centre schedules compute against power and thermal rejection capacity. Same problem, one level up.

08 · Ask them

Three at most. All of these survive a bounce-back.

  • "Is it single or two-phase, and was mass flow or the glide penalty the deciding argument?"
  • "With 800 plates in parallel, how do you guarantee the hottest card isn't the starved one? I built a 1D manifold model and maldistribution off a shared header was what surprised me."
  • "I spent two years on boiling heat transfer and surface geometry. Does the microgravity CHF penalty show up in your testing, or is there enough margin that it doesn't drive anything?"
  • "How do you validate a megawatt-class loop on the ground when you can't reproduce the flow regimes?"
  • "Two thousand square metres for five years — is the loop segmented against micrometeoroid puncture, or designed to tolerate losing a section?"
  • "Is compute load scheduled against the thermal envelope, or treated as fixed?"
