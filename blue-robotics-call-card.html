Tony White · Blue Robotics · Applications Engineer

He is customer-facing. Ask what he sees in the field, not what the company should do.

Open — 20 seconds, then stop

“Hi Tony, thanks for making time. I’m a mechanical engineer in Seattle, wrapping up a DOE fellowship at MarineSitu — power budgeting and duty cycling for instrumentation on buoys and tidal sites, working out how to keep a sensor package alive on a small solar and battery budget from Hawaii up to Anchorage. Before that I was doing thermal-fluids test engineering: building rigs, instrumenting them, chasing down why the data looked wrong. I’ve been curious about Blue Robotics for a while, and what I most wanted to ask you about is the field side — you see what happens to this gear after it leaves Torrance, and I don’t think many people ask you about that.”

Ask two or three — follow the one he warms to

  1. Field failures. “When something comes back from a customer with a problem — is it usually the hardware itself, or how it got integrated?”
    … I’ve spent a lot of time on rigs where the failure was instrumentation, not physics. Curious what the equivalent is for you.
  2. BlueBoat endurance. “How much of what customers ask you about is endurance — what they can run, and for how long?”
    … I spent the last year doing that arithmetic for buoys. Is it a question people arrive with, or one they discover three weeks in?
  3. Heat in the enclosures. “Are you seeing people put more compute into the watertight enclosures — edge processing, bigger sonar?”
    … I’d assume heat becomes a question at some point. Showing up yet, or still theoretical?
  4. Long deployments. “For gear that sits in the water for months — what degrades first?”
    … I did a couple of years on scaling and anti-fouling surfaces. How much of that is a real operational problem versus something people worry about?

The one offer — only if he opens the door

“That’s the thing I’d most want to work on. The buoy work was the same shape of problem: known generation, unknown loads, and a battery you can’t let fall over. Happy to send you what I built if it’s ever useful.”

Say it once. Don’t say it if he doesn’t open the door.

Close

“This was really useful, thank you. Two things before I let you go — is there anyone on the engineering or test side you’d point me toward? And if someone with my background wanted to be useful at Blue Robotics, what would you want to see from them?”

The second one asks for advice he can give, not a decision he can’t make.

Guardrails

  • No pitching. If a question of mine has a job inside it, cut the job out.
  • Don’t lead with 600 W/cm² or fusion. Wrong world. Marine first; the rest only if asked.
  • Torrance. Have one line ready on location before he has to ask.
  • End five minutes early. Giving time back is the proof it wasn’t wasted.
