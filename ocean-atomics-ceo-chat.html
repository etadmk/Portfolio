Conversation prep · Will Cahill, Ocean Atomics

He is not a
nuclear engineer

Public sources describe William Cahill as a co-founder and president of Applied Maritime Sciences, a maritime technology and strategy consultancy. He spent twenty years as a Coast Guard officer — cutterman and search-and-rescue helicopter pilot — then served as Director for Strategic Planning on the National Security Council and Maritime Advisor on the Council of Economic Advisers, working on American maritime competitiveness. His degrees are in naval architecture and marine engineering from the Coast Guard Academy, plus a Princeton MPP.

01 · Why that changes everything

Three of your four drafted questions are aimed at the wrong person. That is fixable and worth ten minutes.

Ocean Atomics has a nuclear technologist on the team — the site describes someone with a Michigan nuclear PhD and fifteen years at TerraPower. Reactor physics questions belong to that person. Cahill's domain is the thing that has actually killed every previous civilian nuclear ship: regulation, liability, port access, crewing, and shipping economics. He has worked the Coast Guard side from inside the Coast Guard and the policy side from inside the White House. That is an unusual and specific expertise, and asking him about coolant chemistry wastes it.

Rewrite — question 1
"Given your broad expertise in nuclear engineering... why does the fusion industry avoid marketing itself as nuclear?"
The premise is wrong and he will notice. Also, the fusion-marketing question is interesting but it is a dinner-party question, not a question for the CEO of a maritime nuclear company. Spend the slot better. If you want to raise fusion at all, raise it as your own background in one clause and move on.
Rewrite — question 3
"As someone who has studied and catalogued so many concepts, what is your all-time favourite reactor design?"
This is aimed at the reactor cataloguer on the team, not at Cahill. Asked of him it reads as if you have not looked him up. The equivalent question for him is about ships, not reactors — see the "favourite" question below, which keeps the warmth and lands on his actual expertise.
Rewrite — question 4
"What are the trade-offs of water versus liquid metal coolant?"
Keep the instinct, change the frame. He will not answer this as a thermal-hydraulicist; he will answer it as someone choosing a licensable technology. Ask why water-cooled was the right call for regulatory and crewing reasons, and you get a far better answer plus a natural opening for your Zap experience.

Question 2 — why maritime and nuclear go together — is well aimed. Keep it.

02 · The regulatory stack he lives in

Hold this in your head and you can follow anything he says.

Classification societies sit alongside this — ABS, DNV, Lloyd's — and the Approval in Principle process is how concepts currently get validated. ABS granted an AIP in July 2026 to a Korean 15,000 TEU container ship design using two marine molten salt reactors, which tells you the competitive landscape is moving.

What just happened, three days before your chat

The IAEA launched its ATLAS initiative — Atomic Technologies Licensed for Applications at Sea — in Washington on 26–27 August 2026, with a US industry day on the 25th. It is explicitly aimed at clearing regulatory barriers for civilian reactors on ships and floating platforms. Given his background and their location, there is a strong chance he was in the room. Ask about it. It is current, it is his lane, and it signals you follow the industry rather than just the company.

03 · The history that explains the company

Four civilian nuclear ships have sailed. Knowing why they stopped is the best possible grounding for this conversation.

That pattern is the entire thesis of a company like Ocean Atomics. The technology worked. What failed was economics, port acceptance and the absence of a liability framework. Russia sustained a fleet because Arctic icebreaking had no alternative and the state absorbed the cost. So the interesting question is what has changed — decarbonisation pressure, fuel cost, SMR standardisation, or the regulatory push ATLAS represents. That is the question he will most want to answer.

04 · Your questions, rebuilt

Pick four. Lead with the one that fits the moment.

"Every civilian nuclear ship before this failed on economics and port access rather than on the reactor. What's actually different now — is it decarbonisation pressure, SMR standardisation, or the regulatory work like ATLAS?"
The best opener you have. It shows you know the history, it is genuinely his question, and it invites the story he most wants to tell.
"ATLAS launched in Washington last week. Were you there, and did it change your read on the timeline?"
Current, specific, and low-risk. If he was there you get the inside version; if not, you still get his assessment.
"You've worked the Coast Guard side from inside and the policy side from the NSC. Where does the real bottleneck sit — NRC licensing of the plant, Coast Guard acceptance of the vessel and crew, or port states deciding whether to let it dock?"
This is the question only he can answer, and the three-way framing shows you understand the stack. Expect a long answer.
"What's the appeal of putting the reactor on a ship rather than on land — is it manufacturing repeatability, unlimited cooling water, or getting around siting and permitting?"
Your question 2, essentially unchanged. It works.
"You've gone water-cooled while some competitors are pursuing molten salt. From where you sit, how much of that was a licensability and crew-training decision rather than a thermal one?"
Your question 4, re-aimed. This gives him a strategy question instead of a physics question, and it opens the door for you to mention Zap in his terms rather than yours.
"Training or creating licensed operators is unusual for a reactor company to take on. Is the crewing pipeline as hard as the licensing?"
Their own materials say they will train operators. It is a real commitment and almost nobody asks about it. Merchant marine crewing is squarely his world.
"What's your favourite ship — the one you always want to talk about?"
Your question 3, redirected from reactors to vessels. Same warmth, right target. Twenty years at sea and a naval architecture degree; he has an answer.

05 · The one technical question only you can ask

Save this for when the conversation is going well. It is the strongest thing you own.

"A ship is a time-varying gravity vector. Natural circulation and passive safety systems are driven by ρgΔh, and heave changes g by ten percent or more at a fraction of a hertz. My background is two-phase flow and critical heat flux experiments — how much does ship motion actually shape the thermal-hydraulic design?"
Nobody else asks this. It comes straight from your UIUC and UCSD work, it is a real open problem in marine reactor design, and even if he hands it to the nuclear lead, he will remember that you asked it.

06 · Context primers

Tap any of these if the term is fuzzy. You do not need to raise them; you need to not be lost if he does.

07 · Your bridges

  • You already work in marine energy. A DOE ORISE fellow with the Water Power Technologies Office, doing field-deployable ocean power systems. That is the single most relevant line on your résumé for this company and it is current. Lead with it.
  • Your boiling work is reactor thermal hydraulics. Departure from nucleate boiling is the limiting phenomenon in a pressurised water reactor — DNBR is the core safety margin. You ran CHF experiments and studied how surface geometry changes nucleation. Say it plainly: "my experimental background is critical heat flux, which I understand is the same limit that sets DNBR."
  • Fouling and seawater. Your falling-film desalination work was literally seawater scaling on heat transfer surfaces. Any marine plant has a seawater-cooled condenser with exactly that problem.
  • Zap. Mention it as nuclear-adjacent materials and liquid metal loop work, briefly, and let him ask. Do not oversell fusion at a fission company.
  • The MarineSitu line you drafted is good. "Characterised the power system from field data, built the forecasting and optimisation, validated both against telemetry, and defined what a live deployment loop would look like — setting my own roadmap and coordinating hardware specs along the way." Tighten to: characterised, modelled, validated, and scoped the deployment. The self-directed part is the part that matters to a CEO.

08 · Do not do these

  • Do not call him a nuclear engineer or imply he catalogues reactors. Both are someone else on his team.
  • Do not lead with fusion. At a fission company it can read as tourism. One clause, in passing, only if it is relevant to the answer.
  • Do not litigate nuclear public opinion or bring up accidents unprompted. He has heard it and it burns a slot.
  • Do not ask anything answerable from their homepage.
  • Do not ask for a job in the first ten minutes. If it goes well, close with "who else should I be talking to?" — which is a better ask anyway.
